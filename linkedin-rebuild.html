The launch post

Launch posts usually fail because they announce rather than argue. "Excited to share that I'm starting…" gives a reader nothing to react to, so nobody does.

This one opens on a claim strange enough to earn the next line, spends its middle on a problem your audience recognizes in themselves, and closes with two asks — one for people who have the problem, one for people who know somebody who does. The second ask is what generates comments, because it's easy to answer.

Launch post — post today ~1,800 chars · no hashtags, no link

The last thing I deliver is the person who replaces me.

That's not a tagline. It's the scope of work.

After 23 years in sales — carrying numbers up to $12M — I'm starting something of my own. Here's the problem I'm building it around.

A founder builds something good. They sell it brilliantly, because nobody believes in it the way they do. Then they hit a ceiling somewhere between $2M and $10M that they can't sell their way through.

So they hire reps.

And the reps inherit a process that exists nowhere except the founder's head. No ICP. No qualification framework. A CRM that's a graveyard. Twelve months later two people are gone, and the founder quietly concludes the market requires them personally.

It doesn't. Nobody ever built the system.

That's the six months I do. ICP, the narrative, the cadences, the process, the tech stack, the CRM actually live and actually used.

And then — this is the part people find strange — I run the search for their VP of Sales, hand that person a company that runs without me, and I leave.

The average fractional sales engagement runs 9.7 months and drifts. Of course it does. Nobody sitting in that seat is paid to end it.

I'd rather be measured on whether you still needed me in month seven.

Two places I'm deepest. Digital health, where I've watched good reps run a twelve-month committee sale like a sixty-day deal. And commercial construction, where I've watched someone who's never stood on a jobsite lose the room in ten minutes.

If you're the best salesperson in your own company and you know that's a problem — I'd like to talk.

And if someone came to mind while you were reading this: that's the intro I'd be grateful for.

No hashtags — they read as marketing and don't help reach on a personal post. No external link in the body; LinkedIn suppresses those. If you want to point somewhere, put it in the first comment.

How to work the post

  • Post Tuesday–Thursday, 7:30–9:00am Eastern. Not Monday, not Friday.
  • Clear two hours after posting. Reply to every comment within the first ninety minutes — early comment velocity is most of what drives distribution, and a reply counts as engagement.
  • Reply with substance, not thanks. Every response should add a sentence someone else would want to read. "Thanks Dave!" is a wasted slot.
  • Send it to 20 people directly before you post publicly, with one line: "Going out tomorrow — wanted you to see it first." That's a network reactivation touch and a distribution mechanism in the same message.
  • Don't edit it after posting. Edits reset distribution.
  • Pin it to Featured once it's up.

Cold LinkedIn outreach

LinkedIn DMs work on completely different physics than email. The connection note caps at 300 characters, everyone can see you looked at their profile, and the medium is far less formal. Long messages read as automation and get ignored.

So the note doesn't pitch. It earns the connection. The pitch waits until they've accepted — which is a yes, and yes is worth waiting for.

Connection note — construction 212 chars · 300 limit

[Name] — saw you're hiring AEs with no sales leader in place yet. That gap is the thing I get called for. 23 years selling, a lot of it in construction and facilities. Not pitching you — happy to just connect.

"Not pitching you" is load-bearing. It's true at this stage, and it roughly doubles accept rate versus a note that opens with what you do.

Connection note — digital health 227 chars · 300 limit

[Name] — saw the AE opening, and that there's no sales leader above it yet. That's the moment I usually get called. 23 years selling, most recently in digital health. Not pitching — happy to just be connected.

Swap the opening line to whatever signal is actually true: a recent raise, a new segment, a stale VP req. Never send a signal line you haven't verified.

No signal? Use this instead 198 chars

[Name] — I work with [digital health / contech] founders making the move out of founder-led sales. Yours is one of the companies I've been watching. Would like to be connected either way.

Weaker, and that's fine — it's honest. Far better than inventing a signal, which is the one mistake that permanently costs you a future buyer.

After they accept — wait 2 days the actual message

Thanks for connecting, [Name].

Since I've got you — the reason your AE search caught my eye is that hiring reps before there's a sales leader is usually the moment a company loses a year. The reps inherit a process that only exists in the founder's head, most of them wash out, and it reads as a hiring problem when it's a system problem.

That's the six months I do. ICP, the story, cadences, process, CRM — then I hire the VP of Sales who takes it over and I'm gone.

Worth 20 minutes? And if the timing's wrong, genuinely no problem — I'll leave you alone.

Under 120 words. Two days after the accept, not the same minute — an instant follow-up is the signature of automation and everyone recognizes it.

Close-out — 10 days later then stop

[Name] — closing the loop so I'm not cluttering your inbox. If founder-led sales is still where your week goes six months from now, I'm easy to find. If it isn't, that's the better outcome.

Two messages after the accept, then stop. An unanswered founder is a future buyer, not a target to wear down.

Volume ceiling

Keep connection requests under 20 per day and personalize every one. LinkedIn restricts accounts that run high-volume generic invites, and a restricted account costs you the channel entirely. Twenty personalized notes a day is 100 a week — well past the playbook's 15 new companies.
