# LinkedIn post — the altered photo

## POSTED 2026-09-17 — this is the version that went out

Her own draft. The three below are what I offered and she rejected; they're kept
only as a record of what didn't work. The lesson is written up as a copy rule in
CLAUDE.md — short version: on a personal post, drop the credentials, the thesis,
the instructions to the reader and the business adjacency. Authority came from
declining to claim any.

> I've spent a lot of time debating whether I should post this. I kept thinking "Is LinkedIn the place for this?" and "Does it even matter?" but I kept coming back to the fact that we, as women, have a responsibility to each other — especially in the workplace — to talk about things that aren't okay when they happen.
>
> A company I used to work for is hosting an event in a few weeks and recently started promoting it with a team photo that I was in. I say "was" because instead of cropping me out or simply choosing another photo, they used AI to generate a completely different face and put it over mine.
>
> It's still my body. My posture. My hands. The clothes I picked out and put on that morning. But when you look at the picture, someone else's face is where mine used to be. And, truth be told, I've been trying to figure out why it bothered me as much as it did.
>
> I think there's something very different about being removed from a picture and being replaced in one. If I had simply been cropped out, I honestly don't know that I would have thought much about it. But there's something incredibly unsettling about keeping everything else about me in the photo while using AI to make it look like I was never actually there.
>
> We spend so much time talking about whether AI is going to replace people's jobs, but we're not talking nearly enough about the smaller, more human decisions it's suddenly making incredibly easy. Companies have always been able to control the story they tell after someone leaves. What's different now is that you can literally rewrite who was standing in the room. A few minutes with AI and the person is gone while everything around them stays exactly where it was.
>
> Women being labeled difficult, pushed out and then quietly written out of the story certainly isn't new. AI didn't create that. It just made it possible to do with a prompt.

What survived from the drafts: the removed-vs-replaced distinction, the posture/
hands/clothes detail, and the closing move on AI making an old thing cheap. What
she cut: every credential, every prescription, and the optional block naming the
retaliation.

## A — The photograph (full version, ~2,000 chars)

> A company I used to work for is hosting an event in a few weeks. They're promoting it with a team photo I'm in.
>
> Except I'm not in it anymore.
>
> They didn't crop me out. They didn't choose a different picture. They used AI to generate a new face and put it over mine.
>
> My posture. My hands. The clothes I picked out that morning. Someone else's face.
>
> I've been trying to work out why that landed harder than the leaving did, and I think this is it: cropping someone out is an edit. This is a replacement. The work stays in the frame. The person doesn't.
>
> Here's the part I'd want other leaders to actually sit with. The instinct isn't new. Companies have always wanted to keep the story and lose the person. What's new is that it now takes a few minutes and leaves no seam. No photographer to ask. No awkward gap in the lineup. Nobody in the room saying "wait, wasn't someone standing there?" All the friction that used to make somebody stop and think about what they were doing — that's gone.
>
> We spend a lot of time asking whether AI is going to take people's jobs. Less time on the quieter thing it's already doing: making it effortless to erase the people whose jobs it didn't take.
>
> Every organization I sell into has an AI policy. It covers customer data. It covers what you can paste into a chatbot. It covers disclosure. I have never once seen one that covers whether you can synthesize a former employee's face onto her own body in a photograph she agreed to be in.
>
> Somebody should write that policy. It's going to matter sooner than people think.
>
> [OPTIONAL BLOCK — see note]
>
> Women have been told they were difficult and then quietly written out of the story for as long as there have been stories. What's changed is that it no longer takes a story. It takes a prompt.

**The optional block.** Insert before the final paragraph if she wants the reason
stated:

> I'll say the rest of it plainly, because I think it's the real subject. I raised a concern at that company about how a colleague spoke to me and to people who reported to me. I'm not there anymore. I'm not going to relitigate that here and I'm not naming anyone — that isn't the point and it wouldn't help.

This is the sentence that turns an AI-ethics post into a public account of a
retaliation sequence. It's also what makes the post true rather than tasteful.
Her call, made knowingly — and worth a look at the separation agreement first.

## B — The policy question (~1,500 chars, aimed at her buyers)

Lowest exposure, highest shareability inside HR and benefits. Gives readers
something to do, which is what drives comments.

> Your AI policy covers customer data. Does it cover your employees' faces?
>
> Genuine question. I'm asking because of something that happened to me this week.
>
> A company I used to work for is promoting an upcoming event with a team photo I'm in. Or was in. They used AI to generate a new face and put it over mine. Same body, same posture, same room. Different face.
>
> Nobody had to ask me. Nobody had to ask the photographer. It took minutes and cost nothing, and I only know because I happened to see it.
>
> I've sold into HR and benefits organizations for eight years. Every one of them is having the AI conversation right now — productivity, customer data, what's allowed in a prompt. Almost none of them have written down the employee side of it. Whose likeness is it. What happens to it when that person leaves. Who signs off before a real face gets synthesized into a marketing asset.
>
> Three questions worth putting to your team this quarter:
>
> Do we have a written rule about altering photographs of real employees?
>
> Who approves it, and does the person in the photograph get told?
>
> Does that rule still apply after someone leaves?
>
> If the answer to the first one is no, you don't have an AI problem yet. You have a respect problem that AI is about to make very cheap.
