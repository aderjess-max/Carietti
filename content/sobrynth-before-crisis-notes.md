## What changed and why

1. **The $100,000 stands alone on its own line.** It was buried mid-sentence. It's the most
   arresting thing in the piece and it now gets the beat it deserves, three lines in.
2. **Added the decision behind the claim.** "By the time a number like that reaches a benefits
   team, the decision that produced it was made months earlier — at a kitchen table, near
   midnight, by someone with no training in any of this." That line does the whole argument's work
   and gives the piece its spine.
3. **Pulled in Sobrynth's own numbers from the overview deck**, so nothing here is a claim the
   company doesn't already stand behind: two in three American families directly affected by
   someone's substance use (SAMHSA NSDUH), and the NSC/NORC employer cost modeling in the byline.
   The two-in-three figure lands twice on purpose — once at the pivot to families, once in the
   closing question, where it converts a rhetorical question into a settled fact.
4. **"The claim is the last cost, not the first."** New line, and the hinge of the cost section.
5. **Named the failure correctly.** "That isn't a failure of judgment. It's a failure of
   navigation. Nobody handed them a map." The original left the family sounding careless; this
   moves the fault to the system, which is both fairer and more persuasive to an HR reader who
   *is* the system.
6. **Added a guardrail paragraph — the most important edit here.** Written as-is, a piece arguing
   that families default to expensive residential care reads dangerously close to cost containment
   in disguise, and recovery-community readers will catch that instantly. The new paragraph says
   plainly that peer specialists don't authorize, deny, or gatekeep, that no claim is filed and no
   diagnosis is shared, and that sometimes inpatient is exactly right. It protects the argument
   *and* it's a genuine differentiator — "no claims filed" is a strange and powerful thing to be
   able to say in a piece about a claim.
7. **Added post-discharge support** to the inpatient case, from the deck's peer-support multiplier.
   Without it the piece implies Sobrynth's value ends once a level of care is chosen.
8. **"The ninety days nobody owns."** The original's reactive-vs-proactive section was abstract.
   Naming the window makes it something a benefits leader can picture owning.
9. **Six section headers**, and roughly 15% of the words cut.

Four tags, all specific to the buyer, all at the very bottom — never mid-sentence, which
hyperlinks words and pulls readers out of the copy. Nothing broad like #Leadership.

**The page post leads with Olympia Hospitality**, so their written sign-off matters more here than
it does in the article. Without it, open on "One of our employer partners in hospitality noticed
something most employers miss" and the post works unchanged.

## Mechanics

**Stagger them.** The first article went out from both accounts simultaneously; there is no upside
to running that experiment twice. Personal post first in the Tue–Thu 7:30–9:00am ET window, page
post 60–90 minutes behind. Comment on the page post from her personal profile inside the first
hour — substantive, and not the same text used anywhere else.

Never edit after posting. Reply to every comment inside 90 minutes on both.
