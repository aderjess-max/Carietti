# Before Crisis Becomes a Claim

It landed on an HR leader's desk like any other high-cost claim.

$100,000.

The claim wasn't for an employee. It was for their spouse.

## What the claim doesn't show

By the time a number like that reaches a benefits team, the decision that produced it was made months earlier — at a kitchen table, near midnight, by someone with no training in any of this.

Behind every large claim is a family in crisis and an employee trying to hold it together. Taking calls between meetings. Leaving early for appointments. Reading plan documents at 11pm. Wondering whether they're making the right decision for someone they love, with no real way to know.

Even when they're at their desk, most of their attention is somewhere else.

Two in three American families are directly affected by someone's substance use. Your benefits data can see almost none of it — until it arrives as a claim.

## The costs start long before treatment does

There's the claim itself. Then the productivity lost while an employee tries to navigate a system nobody taught them to read. The absenteeism. The quiet toll on managers and coworkers carrying someone through the hardest season of their life.

The claim is the last cost, not the first.

What comes before it is a search. A few hours online, a recommendation from a friend, and a family lands on the most visible option — usually residential care — without ever learning what else exists or what actually fits their situation.

That isn't a failure of judgment. It's a failure of navigation. Nobody handed them a map.

## What one employer did differently

One of our employer partners saw it plainly: their employees and families didn't need another phone number to call. They needed someone to walk beside them.

Through Sobrynth, families get confidential, one-on-one support from peer specialists — people in recovery themselves — who help them understand the treatment landscape, navigate insurance, and find the level of care that's clinically appropriate for their situation.

Sometimes that's inpatient treatment. When it is, the family gets there faster and better prepared, and someone stays with them after discharge, which is where recovery is usually won or lost.

Other times, outpatient or intensive outpatient care provides the right support while keeping someone connected to work, family, and community — at a fraction of the cost of residential care.

Let me be direct about what this is not. Our peer specialists don't authorize, deny, or gatekeep anything. This is not utilization management with a warmer voice. No claim is filed to reach us, no diagnosis is shared, and no employee data goes back to the employer. We're the people a family talks to *before* they have to choose.

The difference isn't which option a family lands on. It's that they aren't landing on it alone.

## The ninety days nobody owns

Every benefits strategy has an answer for the six-figure claim. Almost none has an answer for the ninety days before it.

That window is where the cost is actually decided — and it's the window where a family is most alone and least equipped. Employers can be present in it. Most simply aren't yet.

When they are, the results follow: better-informed decisions, a workforce that feels supported rather than managed, and care that matches the need instead of matching whatever surfaced first in a search.

## The real question

The question isn't whether your employees' families will face substance use. Two in three already have.

The question is whether anyone will be walking beside them before a crisis becomes a claim.
