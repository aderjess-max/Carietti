# Guidance Instead of Guesswork

A high-performing employee took leave under FMLA.

Not because she was sick.

Because her son was.

Her college-aged son had started struggling with substance use, and overnight she was asked to become five things no parent is ever prepared to be: care navigator, insurance expert, treatment researcher, crisis manager, and emotional support system.

She had the leave. What she didn't have was a plan.

## The second job nobody applies for

Should he go to inpatient treatment? Outpatient? Did he need treatment yet, or was it too early to tell? What would insurance actually cover? Which of these programs is legitimate, and which one just bought the top search result? What happens if she chooses wrong?

Hours online. Calls to providers who never called back. Hold music. A list of facilities she had no way to evaluate and no one to evaluate it with.

Somewhere in there, the searching stopped being a task and became a full-time job — one with no training, no colleagues, and a deadline she couldn't see.

So she stepped away from the job she was actually good at.

## What it costs the employer

The impact reaches well past one person's absence. Paid leave. Temporary coverage. Delayed projects. Teams absorbing the work, managers redistributing it, coworkers filling the gaps.

Then the second bill arrives.

A family navigating alone tends to default to the most *visible* level of care rather than the most *appropriate* one — usually the most intensive and most expensive, chosen under pressure by people with no way to compare options. The health plan absorbs that decision. The National Safety Council and NORC at the University of Chicago estimate an untreated substance use disorder costs an employer roughly $8,500 a year per affected person, and their calculator counts dependents alongside employees, because the costs never stop at the badge.

None of that shows up on a dashboard labeled "substance use." It shows up as leave, as attrition, as a claim nobody saw coming.

## The people your benefits aren't built for

Most benefits are designed around the person with the diagnosis.

But 46% of U.S. adults say a family member or close friend has been addicted to drugs (Pew Research Center). In any workforce, the parents, partners, and siblings carrying someone else's crisis far outnumber the employees currently in treatment.

They rarely appear in a benefits report. They appear in leave requests, missed deadlines, and resignations coded as "personal reasons."

That's the equation Sobrynth changes.

## Someone who has walked it, walking beside them

Rather than leaving a parent to figure it out alone, our certified peer recovery coaches walk beside them — people with lived experience, available 24/7, confidentially, with no session limits.

We support the employee.

We support the person they love.

And we help both of them navigate the system together.

In practice, that means researching treatment options, explaining the levels of care in plain language, helping a family understand what actually fits their situation, coordinating the next step, and still being on the phone in week eleven — not just week one.

Because our support is uncapped and extends to employees' loved ones, no one has to choose between caring for their family and holding onto their job.

## We meet people where they are

Sometimes that means helping a parent support a child who isn't ready for treatment yet.

Sometimes it means finding a real alternative to residential care.

Sometimes it means giving a family one knowledgeable person to call before they make a decision they can't undo.

The goal was never to move everyone toward the most intensive option.

The goal is to help each person find the right one.

## When the guessing stops

Employees stop losing weeks to a fragmented system. Managers know their people have somewhere real to go. Families get to focus on recovery instead of logistics.

And the employee who would have disappeared for twelve weeks stays connected — supported rather than sidelined.

Substance use is never one person's condition. It's a household's. When the household is supported, employees can care for the people they love and stay connected to the work that depends on them.
