The $400K Slot Math

Three concurrent engagements. Six months each. The number that decides whether this works isn't your rate — it's how many months of the year your three slots sit empty. And the transition off your current 1099 work is what determines whether you ever get to fill them at full price.

01

The finding

$400K across three slots is $11,111 per slot per month at perfect utilization. The market average for a fractional sales leader is $11,732/month. So at 100% utilization, your goal is achievable at slightly below market rate.

Nobody runs at 100%. Engagements end, gaps open, replacements take six to ten weeks to sign. A realistic first year from a cold start is about 70% utilization — roughly 25 billable slot-months out of 36.

36 Slot-months of annual capacity (3 slots × 12 months)
~25 Realistic billable slot-months, year one, cold start
$16,000 Required monthly rate per slot at 70% utilization
$96,000 Your core 6-month engagement price
The thing to internalize

Price is not your constraint — pipeline is. At $16K/month you clear $400K with four to five signed engagements. The entire risk of this plan lives in whether you can originate five deals in twelve months from a cold start, not in whether founders will pay your rate. Plan accordingly: spend your energy on origination, and never discount to fill a slot faster.

The bridge is worth about $95K — don't drop it early

Your two current 1099 engagements pay roughly $12.5K/month against a personal floor near $17.5K/month. Two things follow from that, and they point in opposite directions.

First, you're running about $5K/month short right now. The bridge doesn't cover you — it covers about 70% of you. That's real urgency, but it's a narrow gap, and the first 40% payment on a core engagement is $38,400, which covers that shortfall for seven months by itself. One signature resolves the cash question entirely.

Second, and more important: the bridge is the most valuable asset in your first two quarters, and it isn't the money. It's that the money removes the pressure that would otherwise make you take a cheap first deal. Every consultant who prices below their number does it in the ramp months. You have a partial cushion most people don't.

$12.5K Monthly bridge income from current 1099 work
~$5K Monthly gap against your floor, before any new client
~1 slot Capacity the bridge consumes — 10–20 hrs/week of your three-slot total
$75K–$125K What the bridge contributes to year one, depending on how long you hold it

The cost is capacity. At 10–20 hours a week the bridge occupies roughly one of your three slots, so during the overlap you have two slots for new full-price clients, not three. Run the bridge plus two new engagements and you're at roughly 47 hours a week of delivery before any origination time. That's the ceiling, and it's what sets your exit.

Exit trigger — a condition, not a date

Give notice on both bridge engagements the day your second full-price client signs. Not before, not on a calendar date. At that point two engagements at $16K/month is roughly 1.8× your floor, capacity is genuinely exhausted, and your terms are at-will so you can move immediately. Holding past that point costs you the third slot — which is $96K of the year.

Your pricing architecture

Three tiers, priced off scope and stage — never off hours. The middle tier is the one you sell; the outer two exist so the middle looks chosen rather than quoted. Every tier includes the same deliverable set. What changes is company complexity: number of products, segments, existing reps, and CRM state.

Foundation $72K $12,000 / mo × 6

Pre-Series A, under ~$2M revenue. Founder plus maybe one rep. One product, one segment, CRM is a spreadsheet or nothing.

Founder to Team — core $96K $16,000 / mo × 6

$2M–$10M revenue. One to three reps hired reactively, no manager. Founder is still the closer and the bottleneck. This is the deal you're built for.

Scale Engine $120K $20,000 / mo × 6

$10M+, or multi-product, multi-segment, or post-raise with an aggressive plan. Existing team needs restructuring, not just building.

What every tier includes

  • ICP and segmentation — defined, sized, and sourced into a target list, not a slide
  • The story — full sales narrative, pitch deck, objection handling, proof architecture
  • Outbound system — email templates, multi-channel cadences, sequence logic, messaging by persona
  • Sales process — stages, exit criteria, qualification framework, forecast discipline
  • Tech stack — selection, procurement, and implementation; CRM configured and live with pipeline, fields, reporting, and hygiene rules
  • Enablement — onboarding path and ramp plan for the reps who come next
  • The successor — scorecard, comp plan, interview loop, sourcing, and selection of your replacement

Everything built during the engagement transfers to the client on completion. Say that explicitly in the SOW — it removes a standard objection before it's raised.

Payment mechanics

Your 40/30/30 structure is well-suited to a cold start — it front-loads cash at signature, which is exactly what you need. Worked here against the core $96K engagement.

40% $38,400

Due at signature, before kickoff. Non-refundable. Covers discovery, ICP work, and the narrative build — the front-loaded half of your effort.

30% $28,800

Month 3 gate. Tied to a named artifact set: process live, CRM configured, cadences running, first sequences sending.

30% $28,800

Terminal deliverable, client-specific. Default is signed offer accepted by the VP of Sales. Negotiate the trigger per client at signing — never leave it vague.

Two rules that protect this structure. Define the final trigger in the SOW with a date ceiling — "VP hired, or month 7, whichever comes first" — otherwise a slow-hiring client holds 30% of your fee hostage while you carry an empty slot. And bill the milestone, not the calendar: if they delay the search, the clock still runs.

When a founder pushes back on 40% upfront, the concession is a smaller first payment against a shorter first milestone — never a lower total. Moving to 30/30/40 costs you nothing structurally. Moving to $12K/month costs you $24K and resets your anchor with every future client they refer.

Don't discount the first client

The standard advice for a cold start is to price the first engagement low in exchange for a case study. Skip it. That discount is a concession to cash pressure, and your bridge income means you don't have that pressure — you're $5K/month short, not $17.5K short, and one 40% payment closes the gap for most of a year.

Buy the proof with scope instead. Sell the first client the Foundation tier at $72K — full $12K/month rate, smaller build. You get a referenceable case study at an honest price, and you never have to explain to a referred prospect why their friend paid $60K and they're being quoted $96K. Discounting the rate in month three is a decision you pay for through month thirty.

Positioning

The wedge

Every competitor in section three sells "help scaling out of founder-led sales." You sell the same thing with one structural difference worth stating in the first sentence of every conversation:

The line

"I'm hired to make myself unnecessary in six months. The last thing I deliver is the person who replaces me."

The average fractional engagement runs 9.7 months and drifts, because nobody in the seat is paid to end it. Yours ends on a date, with a named successor and a signed offer. For a founder who has been burned by an open-ended consulting relationship — and many have — that's the whole pitch.

Lead with two verticals, not four

Your range across digital healthcare, SaaS, automotive, and commercial construction is real experience but a weak market position — breadth reads as generalist, and generalist gets rate-compared. Lead with the two where your experience is scarce and the buyers are underserved:

  • Digital health — long, committee-heavy, compliance-gated sales cycles that generic SaaS fractional operators consistently misjudge. NYC has a dense cluster of funded companies and a founder population that has watched a generic sales hire fail against hospital procurement.
  • Commercial construction and construction tech — almost entirely unserved by fractional sales leadership, high average contract values, relationship-driven cycles that reward exactly your kind of operator, and it sits in your backyard. Long Island and the outer boroughs are full of contractors and suppliers who have never been sold to by a fractional exec.

Keep SaaS as the pattern language you speak in and automotive as a third card for supplier and dealer-group conversations. But the website, the LinkedIn headline, and the first sentence should name two verticals, not four. You can always take an adjacent deal; you cannot un-say a generalist positioning.

Credentials to lead with

  • $12M carried quota. A hard number almost nobody in the fractional market can match. It answers "have you actually done this at scale" before it's asked.
  • 23 years. Frame as four full market cycles, not as tenure.
  • Long Island base, NYC metro coverage. An advantage, not an apology — you can be on-site weekly, which most remote fractional operators cannot. For construction and healthcare clients especially, showing up in person is a differentiator. Say "NYC metro," not "Long Island," in market-facing copy.

Origination — where five deals come from

You need five signings. Working backward through a realistic consulting funnel with a warm-heavy mix:

150 Targeted conversations initiated over the year
50 Qualified discovery calls — real problem, real budget
15 Proposals issued
5 Signed engagements

That is roughly 13 new conversations and 4 qualified calls per month — sustainable alongside three active engagements only if you protect the time. Block it like a client meeting. The single most common failure mode for a fractional practice is going dark on origination while delivery is busy, then facing an empty slot with an empty pipeline.

The next thirty days

Nothing above matters until the first engagement is signed. You have bridge income and roughly 20–30 unbooked hours a week right now — this is the most build capacity you'll have all year. Spend it. In order:

  • Write the exit trigger on paper: notice goes out the week client two signs. Decide it now, while it's abstract and cheap.
  • Set the price and write it next to the trigger. The highest-value decision in this plan is refusing the first cheap deal.
  • Get documented metrics and a written quote from both current 1099 clients — now, while you're in the seat and well before you give notice.
  • Write the three tiers and the deliverable set into a one-page offer document you can send the same day as a call.
  • Draft the MSA and SOW template — 40/30/30, defined terminal trigger with a date ceiling, IP transfer clause, placement fee disclosed.
  • Write three case studies from your operating history. Real numbers: quota carried, pipeline built, ramp achieved, team scaled.
  • Start the asset library — ICP workbook and cadence templates first, they're what client one will need in week two.
  • Rebuild the LinkedIn headline and about section on the two-vertical wedge and the "I make myself unnecessary" line.
  • Build the 250-name network list, tiered by warmth, with a specific reason-to-reach-out per person in tier one.
  • Build the 40-name VC and PE platform partner list across NYC seed and Series A funds.
  • Send the first 60 network outreaches — individual, specific, no broadcast announcement.
