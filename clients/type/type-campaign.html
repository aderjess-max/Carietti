01 · Diagnosis

What changes from the first two emails

The first two emails are good at the hard part: the problem is vivid and the product is easy to picture. What's missing is a reason to reply today and a next step small enough to say yes to. "How about we team up" and "would you be open to taking a look" both ask the reader to figure out what happens next. Nurse managers won't do that work between huddles.

Ask one easy question

Every follow-up ends with something answerable in one line: a headcount, a name, an address, a number 1–3. Replies drive both orders and inbox placement.

Lead with the sample

A bag in the break room sells itself to the people who'll lobby for it. The sample becomes the main conversion step, not a PS.

Name the buyer, not just the user

The person you emailed often loves it but doesn't hold the money. Recognition committees, practice councils and hospital foundations usually do. Ask for them by name.

Sell a date, not a holiday

Swap "Nurses Week is coming up" for "order by [date] to have them on the unit May 6." A deadline is something they can act on.

Calendar fix: NP Week ran Nov 8–14, so it's past. Drop it from anything going out now. Bulk orders can't arrive before the holidays, so team holiday gifts are out too. The live window is next year's planning: many teams set their recognition plans and budgets now through December, and Nurses Week orders mostly get placed in February and March.

03 · The offer

Make the order easy to place

Prospects need to see exactly what they're buying before they'll reply to a quote request. Set up three packs and one order page, then link the page from every sequence after the first reply.

Starter

Recognition Pack

For a small, specific group: charge nurses, certified nurses, preceptors, a night shift. [10–24 bags · $X each]. Price the whole pack under a typical manager's P-card limit so it can be bought without a PO.

Core

Unit Pack

Every person on the unit. [25–99 bags · $Y each]. Boxed per person with a card from the manager [if available]. This is the default quote.

System

Department or System

Nurses Week across a hospital or system. [100+ · custom pricing]. Split shipping to multiple units, invoice on net 30, vendor setup handled.

Remove the procurement stall up front

  • Have a vendor packet ready to attach: W-9, certificate of insurance, remit-to details, and a one-page product sheet.
  • Accept credit card, P-card, PO and invoice. Say so in the quote.
  • Publish a "need it by" table: the last order date for Nurses Week delivery, Doctors' Day delivery, and so on.
  • Have an answer for cleaning and infection control. Unit leaders will ask whether it can be wiped down or washed. [confirm material and cleaning guidance]
  • Per-shift math for the "too expensive" reply: three 12s a week for 50 weeks is 150 shifts a year. [$price] ÷ 150 = [$ per shift].

The forwardable approval note

Your contact often has to sell this upstairs. Write the justification for them. Send it in Track B when they ask about pricing, and offer it whenever someone says "I'd have to run it by…".

Subject: Nurses Week gift for [unit] — quick approval For Nurses Week I'd like to give everyone on [unit] a Type bag. It's a crossbody bag designed by a physician to hold what we carry every shift: stethoscope, shears, phone, badge, keys, glasses. It replaces overloaded scrub pockets and the bags that get left at the station. It's something the team will use every shift, not a one-day treat. At [$ per person] for [headcount] people, the total is [$ total], and it arrives by [date] if we order by [date]. Every purchase also supports Operation Happy Nurse, a nonprofit started by a nurse to support nurses' mental health. Can I go ahead?

Two warm sources to work first

  • Ask every buyer for one name. After each delivered order, ask who else in the system plans recognition. Nurse leaders move between hospitals and know their counterparts.
  • Partner with Operation Happy Nurse. Ask for an introduction to the hospitals and chapters already in their network, and offer co-branded Nurses Week messaging. A nonprofit introduction gets opened.
10 · Running it

Deliverability and the numbers to watch

Getting into the inbox

  • Hospital email filters are strict. Plain text, one link at most, no attachments in the first touch.
  • Take the embedded GIF out of new-prospect email 2. Use a linked still image or a link to the GIF. Embedded images in cold email hurt inbox placement.
  • Send from a warmed secondary domain, about 30–50 a day per inbox. Verify every new address before it goes in.
  • Send Tue–Thu, 6:30–7:30am in the recipient's time zone, before morning huddle. Test a 2pm slot against it.

Measure replies, not opens

  • Apple Mail opens every message automatically, so open rates are inflated. Judge subject lines by reply rate.
  • A/B one subject line per step, 50/50, and keep the winner after about 100 sends each.
  • Starting targets to beat: 5% reply rate on Track A and C; half of positive replies take a sample; one in three samples becomes an order.
  • Weekly: sends, replies, samples out, quotes out, orders, average order size, and the segment each came from.
11 · Before you send

Checklist

  • Prices and minimums for the three packs, and the per-shift figure.
  • Lead time, and the last order date for each window: Certified Nurses Day, Doctors' Day, Nurses Week.
  • One or two real customer quotes with permission, and any hospital names you're allowed to mention.
  • What Operation Happy Nurse gets per purchase. A specific number lands better than "supports."
  • Customization: logo, names, gift boxing, card insert. If none exist, remove the brackets that mention them.
  • Cleaning and infection-control guidance.
  • The order page link and the vendor packet.
  • Sample stock and who ships them. The Track B timing assumes a sample goes out within two days.
  • Split the contact list into the tracks: replied (Track B), no reply (Track A), bounced or wrong person (fix, then Track C).
